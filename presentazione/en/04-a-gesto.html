Where it comes from · feature A

A · Same question, another way to answer

Telling the choice apart from the gesture and, when the gesture is what fails, offering an easier way to answer. For each choice, the literature that supports it.

Choice and gesture recorded separately

In the platform. For each trial the game saves which object the child chose and, separately, whether the gesture succeeded. A correct object left outside the backpack does not become a wrong choice.

Why: what the literature says. Motor difficulties are common in autism. A meta-analysis of 83 studies finds broad, widespread coordination deficits; from the questionnaires of about 12,000 children and youths, 86.9% are at risk of motor impairment.

Fournier et al., 2010 · Bhat, 2020

How far it goes. The sources do not say how much the gesture weighs in a matching task: that is why the game measures it instead of assuming it.

How the gesture went, in detail

In the platform. The game tells apart a successful gesture, a release near the backpack, a last-moment exit and an interruption, and measures how far from the backpack's edge the object was released.

Why: what the literature says. In children aged 3–6, the precision of tapping and dragging grows with age, and is linked to finger dexterity and to graphomotor and visuospatial skills.

Vatavu, Cramariuc and Schipor, 2015

How far it goes. The study measures touch as such, not within a choice task: it says the gesture matters, not how much.

Two taps or a more tolerant backpack

In the platform. If the choice is right but the gesture fails, the system proposes tapping the object and then the backpack, or widening the area that accepts the release. Objects and correct answer stay the same.

Why: what the literature says. Accessibility guidelines require that anything done by dragging can also be done without dragging. And a more effortful response becomes less frequent: here the principle is used in reverse, making the right answer easier.

WCAG 2.2, criterion 2.5.7 · Friman and Poling, 1995

How far it goes. WCAG covers the web in general, and the review mostly studies increased effort: the application is plausible, not proven.
