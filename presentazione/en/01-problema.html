The game

One «incorrect» that hides two things

In a drag-and-drop matching task, the same «incorrect» record can mean very different things.

Case 1

Picks the wrong object

The list shows the notebook, the child takes the ruler. This is a stimulus control error: discrimination needs work.

Case 2

Picks the right one, but the gesture fails

Takes the notebook and releases it just outside the backpack, or the finger lifts halfway. The choice was correct: it is the gesture that failed.

If the data sheet records only «correct/incorrect», case 2 becomes false data about discrimination.

The consequences are concrete: the program steps back, or prompts are added for a child who had already discriminated.

And case 2 is not rare. Motor coordination difficulties are broad and widespread in autism (Fournier et al., 2010); in a sample of about 12,000 children and adolescents, 86.9% were at risk of motor impairment (Bhat, 2020). In young children, touchscreen accuracy depends precisely on motor skills (Vatavu et al., 2015).
